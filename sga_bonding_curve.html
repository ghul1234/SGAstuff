your capital only sets your position on it.

A continuous, capital-weighted bonding curve — not a fixed-price allotment. Every dollar deployed moves the marginal price along P(S) = P₀ · e^(k·S). Any investment size clears immediately; the curve alone prevents any single buyer from consuming the float.

Investor Queue

Orders fill against the curve in sequence — each investor buys where the last one left off. You are not the only buyer; later investors face a higher price than earlier ones for identical capital.

Selling Pressure

Models holders exiting back into the curve after the full investor queue above settles. Selling burns shares out of circulation and walks the marginal price back down the same curve — the mirror image of buying.

Matches the Yield Day mechanism already locked into the SGA design — 1.6%/month pulled out to the state treasury (the separate 0.0667%/month reinvested back into the system is treated as staying in reserve, so it's not modeled as a drain here).
Extraction compounds monthly: reserve retained = (1 − rate)^months. At 1.6%/mo that's about 82% retained after 1 year (~18% pulled out, tracking the ~20%/yr Yield Day target), and under 40% retained after 5 years — the curve's redemption promise erodes steadily against a fixed monthly draw.

Buy mechanics — the supply is fixed at the configured share count. Price is a pure function of cumulative shares sold, P(S)=P₀·e^(k·S). An order of capital C solves S₁ from S₀ · e^(k·S₀)+C·k/P₀, i.e. shares are integrated continuously along the curve rather than divided at a flat price, so no investor can buy the float at the launch price. Ownership is always shares held ÷ total supply — no cap is imposed; the acquisition cost itself is the only constraint on size.

Sell mechanics — this model treats the curve as symmetric: selling burns shares out of circulation and walks the marginal price back down the identical P(S) formula, the same way it walked up on the buy side. That's a real design choice, not the only option — it means the SGA system itself is the buyer of last resort on every exit, which only works if it's actually holding enough reserve to pay out. The "buyback reserve retained" slider exists because your existing extraction architecture (the monthly Yield Day pulls, state/federal splits, win-tax, etc.) pulls capital out of the system on an ongoing basis — so the reserve backing this curve is very likely less than 100% of gross capital raised in practice, and the reserve check above will start flashing shortfalls once you dial it down. An alternative would be pure peer-to-peer secondary trading (order-book style) with no system buyback obligation at all, which sidesteps the reserve question entirely but gives up the guaranteed-exit-liquidity property. Worth deciding explicitly which model the paper commits to. All figures are simulation output, not investment advice.
